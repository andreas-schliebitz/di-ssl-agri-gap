The size of agricultural image datasets is continuously increasing as new images are collected across different locations, campaigns and seasons. This ongoing data collection can lead to distribution shift, primarily covariate and label shift, arising from plant development, geographic variation, and differences in farming practice. Retraining a classification model from scratch on the entire accumulated dataset whenever new data becomes available is costly and often impractical, particularly when previously collected data may no longer be accessible. A more feasible alternative is to continue pretraining the model on each new batch of data, building upon the foundation established by the preceding model, especially when labeling is expensive. While coarse distinctions, such as differentiating crops from weeds, can be obtained at relatively low cost, identifying individual species requires expert botanical knowledge and is therefore difficult to scale to the large volumes of images typically collected. Self-supervised pretraining, which leverages unlabeled images to train a single encoder that can subsequently be applied to a range of downstream classification tasks, therefore presents an attractive alternative.

However, the impact of self-supervised pretraining on representation quality when performed incrementally on a stream of incoming data, rather than jointly on the entire dataset, remains insufficiently explored. In particular, it is unclear whether this impact depends on the granularity of the available labels. Under joint pretraining, the quality of self-supervised representations is typically assessed using a single benchmark score, which, by its nature, cannot distinguish between performance at coarse and fine-grained levels. We hypothesize that incremental pretraining will produce weaker representations than joint pretraining, but there is currently limited evidence regarding the level of labeling granularity at which such differences emerge or the magnitude of these differences. This study therefore aims to empirically investigate this question.

To this end, we pretrain a self-distilling vision transformer model (DINOv2) on the iNatAg-mini dataset, which comprises 560,846 images of plants representing 2,958 species. We conduct pretraining in two distinct settings: first, jointly on the entire training dataset and second, sequentially, by dividing the data into two, four, or eight segments and performing pretraining in a chained sequence. For the sequential setting, we consider two different data presentation orders. The first maintains consistent class coverage across segments, allowing us to isolate the effect of sequential presentation. The second follows the order of data acquisition by season, accounting for hemisphere and deliberately introducing the types of coverage shifts that can occur in real-world agricultural image datasets. In both the joint and sequential settings, each image is seen the same number of times, ensuring a compute-matched comparison. Each resulting model is subsequently frozen and evaluated using linear probes at four levels of the taxonomic hierarchy: species, genus, family and crop-versus-weed detection. In addition to overall accuracy, we measure the distance between predictions and true labels within the taxonomy, the frequency with which species are misclassified while remaining within the correct genus or family and the degree of representational drift across the different pretraining scenarios.

All results, together with the complete experimental code and configurations required to reproduce our findings, will be made publicly available on GitHub.
